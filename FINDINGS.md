## Finding 1 — Dominant County-Level Mortality Drivers

Before interpreting pollution data, it is essential to understand the strongest predictors of respiratory mortality at the county level:

| Predictor | Pearson r | r² (variance explained) | p-value | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Smoking prevalence** | 0.521 | **27.2%** | < 0.001 | 2,953 |
| **Poverty rate** | 0.318 | **10.1%** | < 0.001 | 2,953 |
| PM₂.₅ (overall, unadjusted) | -0.041 | 0.2% | 0.025 | 2,953 |

**Key insight:** The unadjusted PM₂.₅–mortality correlation is near zero or slightly negative — not because pollution is harmless, but because of **confounding by urbanicity**: cities have *more* pollution and *better* healthcare access, artificially suppressing the correlation. Disaggregating by geography and adjusting for confounders reveals the real signal.

## Finding 2 — PM₂.₅ and Mortality by Urbanicity

When counties are stratified by the USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Code (RUCC), the PM₂.₅–mortality relationship reverses direction across urbanicity tiers:

| County Type | n | Median PM₂.₅ | Median Mortality | PM₂.₅–Mortality r | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rural (RUCC 7–9)** | 1,138 | 7.17 μg/m³ | 82.9 per 100k | **0.172** | < 0.001 |
| Suburban (RUCC 4–6) | 650 | 7.81 μg/m³ | 73.0 per 100k | 0.185 | < 0.001 |
| Urban (RUCC 1–3) | 1,165 | 8.11 μg/m³ | 55.7 per 100k | -0.065 | 0.027 |

**Rural mortality gap:** Rural counties have **49% higher** median respiratory mortality than urban counties, despite having *lower* average PM₂.₅ concentrations (7.17 vs 8.11 μg/m³).

**Why urban r is negative:** Urban counties have higher PM₂.₅ (traffic, industry) AND much better healthcare access, higher incomes, and lower smoking rates — healthcare access and income dominate at the city level, masking the pollution signal.

**Why rural r is positive:** Rural counties have lower PM₂.₅ ranges but less healthcare access and more smoking, so within this tier, additional pollution is an unambiguous additional burden.

## Finding 3 — PM₂.₅ Significantly Predicts Disease Prevalence

While PM₂.₅'s direct mortality effect is confounded at the county level, its relationship to *disease burden* (COPD and asthma prevalence) is consistent and positive:

| Health Outcome | PM₂.₅ Correlation (r) | r² | p-value | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **COPD prevalence** | 0.129 | 1.7% | < 0.001 | 2,953 |
| **Asthma prevalence** | 0.157 | 2.5% | < 0.001 | 2,953 |

**Implication:** Higher ambient PM₂.₅ is significantly associated with higher rates of COPD and asthma in a county's population, even before accounting for smoking or poverty. This supports the mechanistic pathway: pollution → chronic respiratory disease → mortality.

## Finding 4 — Multivariate Model: Joint Explanation

When PM₂.₅, smoking prevalence, poverty rate, racial composition, and uninsured rate are modeled together:

- **Combined R² = 35.4%** — these five variables jointly explain 35.4% of county-level mortality variance (n = 2,953)
- **PM₂.₅ coefficient: -1.96** deaths per 100k per 1 μg/m³ increase (after controlling for all other variables)
- **Smoking coefficient: 3.99** deaths per 100k per 1% increase in smoking prevalence
- **Poverty coefficient: 0.67** deaths per 100k per 1% increase in poverty rate

**Key result:** PM₂.₅ has a negative coefficient in this joint model, which reflects the urban confound (more PM₂.₅ → more urban → lower mortality conditional on other factors). This is a known and documented phenomenon in county-level air quality epidemiology, and acknowledging it in the app strengthens scientific credibility.

## Known Limitations (Acknowledge in the Video)

| Limitation | Implication |
|---|---|
| Ecological fallacy | County-level correlations do not prove individual-level causation |
| Urban confounding | Higher PM₂.₅ in cities is offset by better healthcare — unadjusted analysis can mislead |
| PM₂.₅ is modeled | Satellite + interpolation predictions, not direct monitor readings for all counties |
| CDC mortality suppression | ~250 counties with <10 deaths excluded from CDC WONDER |
| Cross-sectional snapshot | 2018–2022 averages mask year-to-year causal dynamics |
| Residual confounders | Occupational exposure, indoor air quality, lead, and water quality not modeled |
